Summary

This commentary reflects on the evolution and continued relevance of placemaking as both a planning concept and a practical approach to shaping public space. It describes placemaking as a flexible and contested idea that has circulated across disciplines, geographies, institutions, and ideological settings. The paper traces how placemaking has been used by grassroots actors, civic institutions, artists, developers, and local governments, often with different goals and different understandings of public value.

Rather than treating this ambiguity as a weakness, the commentary uses it to examine why placemaking remains influential in contemporary planning. The paper focuses on four current contexts: post-capitalism, post-liberalism, post-pandemic urbanism, and post-truth politics. Each context raises different questions about who makes places, whose knowledge counts, how public space is governed, and whether placemaking can remain inclusive under changing political and economic conditions.

The commentary emphasizes that placemaking can support community engagement, collaboration, and shared urban life, but it can also be appropriated by market actors or institutions in ways that dilute its public purpose. The contribution is synthetic and agenda-setting. It situates placemaking within broader debates about urban crisis, authenticity, democracy, collaboration, public life, and the future of planning practice, while arguing that the concept remains useful precisely because it can be reinterpreted in response to changing social and spatial conditions.
