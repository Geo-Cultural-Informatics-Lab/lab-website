Summary

This short paper investigates the use of large language models for synthetic mobility generation under conditions where observed trajectories are costly, sparse, noisy, or privacy-sensitive. It asks whether LLMs can do more than produce plausible individual routes: whether they can also generate profile-specific transition structures across urban buildings. The paper compares agents with and without a car under two regimes.

In the supervised regime, generation is constrained by a gravity-based transition matrix. In the autonomous regime, the model relies more heavily on building attributes and prompt context. Results from 4,000 generated trajectories in Jerusalem show that both profile and autonomy affect trajectory length, travel distance, land-use composition, and transition-graph connectivity.

The supervised mode improves spatial coherence by reproducing gravity-like distance and building-size patterns, but also introduces constraint-related errors, especially around returning home. The autonomous mode shows that LLMs can adjust mobility distributions to transport mode, but still requires stronger behavioral constraints. The broader contribution is exploratory: it identifies where LLM-based trajectory generation is promising for urban simulation, and where it needs structured spatial guidance, controlled rule flexibility, and validation against real-world data.
