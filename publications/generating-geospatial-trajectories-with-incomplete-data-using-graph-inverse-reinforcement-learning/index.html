Summary

This paper studies how realistic geospatial trajectories can be generated under conditions of incomplete and mismatched data. Urban simulations often require detailed daily trajectories, but the empirical datasets available for constructing those trajectories are rarely complete. The paper addresses this problem by combining a GPS-based travel survey from Jerusalem with a GIS dataset of all buildings.

Because the two datasets do not align perfectly, the authors use information propagation through multidimensional clustering to transfer relevant attributes and reduce the mismatch between mobility observations and spatial entities. The modeling framework uses inverse reinforcement learning to infer rewards from observed movement behavior, then uses those rewards to generate trajectories. The paper compares several reward-function approximators: a linear baseline, a multilayer perceptron, and graph neural networks.

The graph-based models are designed to capture the network structure of transitions between urban locations, allowing rewards to reflect both local neighborhood structure and broader spatial patterns. The results show that graph-derived rewards relate more strongly to building floor space and produce different transition probabilities than simpler baselines. The paper also tests multiple initial and terminal state configurations and evaluates generated trajectories through aggregate indicators rather than exact individual-level similarity, which is appropriate when the goal is realistic population-level behavior under data constraints.

The broader contribution is to GeoAI and spatial simulation: it shows how graph inverse reinforcement learning can support trajectory generation when detailed mobility data are scarce, incomplete, or difficult to align with urban geodata.
