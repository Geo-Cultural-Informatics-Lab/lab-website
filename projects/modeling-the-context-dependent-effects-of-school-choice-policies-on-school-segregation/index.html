Overview

This project uses agent-based modeling to examine how school choice policies affect socioeconomic and racial segregation in urban school systems. School choice reforms are often promoted as a way to expand educational opportunity, but they may also intensify segregation when families' school preferences interact with residential patterns, school quality, peer composition, and admission rules. Rather than focusing on a single case study, the project treats the school system as a complex urban system whose outcomes depend on feedback between parental choices, school composition, and initial demographic conditions.

The project develops a synthetic urban school model in which students, households, and schools interact across repeated yearly rounds of school assignment. It compares several policy mechanisms, including pure school choice, distance-priority controlled choice, and a controlled choice policy with a soft quota for low socioeconomic status students. These policies are tested across multiple starting conditions, including different racial compositions, socioeconomic distributions, and levels of residential and school segregation.

The simulations show that the effects of school choice are highly context dependent. No single policy produces a universal outcome: the direction and magnitude of segregation change depend on the interaction between the policy mechanism and the system's initial conditions. Distance-priority policies tend to preserve existing segregation patterns, while pure choice and low-SES quota policies can produce more dynamic outcomes, including both desegregation and, under some conditions, increased segregation.

The project contributes to policy analysis by showing how geosimulation and complexity thinking can help evaluate educational reforms before implementation, especially when their effects are likely to vary across urban contexts.
